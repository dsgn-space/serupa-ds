# Design principles

Five principles guide how Serupa's components, tokens and docs are made, and how the screens built with them should behave. Use them to decide cases a guide doesn't cover, and to settle design reviews.

They are for weighing choices, not a checklist to tick. When they conflict, accessibility comes first.

## Purposeful

Each screen has one clear job. Show what serves that job, in a clear order, and leave the rest out.

Every extra button, option or sentence is one more thing to read and skip. A focused screen can be understood at a glance.

- Open on the task or the content, not on a welcome screen, a tour or a banner.
- Start from who the screen is for and what they came to do, before choosing a single component.
- One primary action per view. A second job waits until the first is done well.
- Simple is not bare. Show the few things people use most, and the rest one step away, in an order that says where people are and what comes next.
- Use the fewest, plainest words. Label actions with what they do: "Delete project", not "OK". See [Content and voice](?path=/docs/foundations-content-and-voice--docs).

## Trustworthy

Tell people what is really happening, never waste their time or work, and handle their data as if it were your own.

Trust builds slowly and breaks at once. A progress bar that lies, a flow with no way out, a mistake that can't be undone, or a cancel button hidden five screens deep teaches people to doubt everything else the product says.

- Show what is really happening: respond to input at once, report progress that reflects real work, and never say "Saved" before the save finishes.
- Make mistakes cheap. Act at once and offer Undo rather than asking "Are you sure?"; confirm only what can't be undone. See [Destructive actions](?path=/docs/guides-destructive-actions--docs).
- Never trap people in a flow. A tour, an onboarding checklist or a multi-step form can be left, skipped or finished later, and Back, Cancel and Escape return people to where they were with their work intact.
- Ask only for what the task needs, and say why before you ask: next to a field that isn't obvious, and before the browser's prompt for the camera, location or notifications. See [Permission request](?path=/docs/patterns-permission-request--docs).
- Make the private choice the default, and leaving as easy as joining: cancelling, unsubscribing and deleting an account take no more steps than signing up.

## Predictable

Build on what people already know from the web and from their devices, and solve the same problem the same way everywhere.

Every new pattern is something people have to learn. Familiar controls and conventions let them use a screen they have never seen, and a control that looks and behaves the same in every place lets what they learn once carry everywhere.

- Use native elements first: `<select>`, `<dialog>`, `<input>`. They bring the phone's picker, autofill and screen-reader support with them.
- Follow the conventions people know: × closes, Escape cancels, Mod+Z undoes, the main action comes last. A familiar pattern beats a clever one.
- Solve the same problem the same way. Components use tokens, never raw values; similar components share sizes, states, keys and words.
- Show what can be used and what just changed: a control looks pressable, a disabled one says why, and a change on screen is announced.

## Inclusive

Everyone can use it, whatever their device, screen size, connection, input method or ability.

People use products with a mouse, a finger, a keyboard, a voice or a screen reader, on a large monitor or an old phone on a slow network. Designing for that range from the start makes the product better for everyone, and it is far cheaper than fixing it later.

- Every component meets WCAG 2.2 AA in every theme, and tests check it. Plan for it from the first sketch.
- Color is never the only signal. A status has a word; a chart series has a shape.
- Every action works with a mouse, touch, a keyboard and voice. Targets are at least 24px, and 44px on touch screens.
- Keep things in their place as the screen changes, and bring people back to where they were. See [Keeping state](?path=/docs/patterns-page-shell--docs).
- Ship only what the page needs, so it is quick on a slow phone too: no runtime dependencies beyond React, a size budget per component, and icons and fonts that load only what's used.

## Crafted

Care about every detail and every state, so the product feels solid and people leave feeling capable rather than processed.

People rarely name the details, but they feel them: a field that lines up, an error that helps, an empty screen that shows the way. Quality in the small moments is what makes a product feel trustworthy and pleasant, and it is never finished at release.

- Each component covers rest, hover, focus, pressed, invalid, read-only, disabled, loading and empty, at every size and in every theme.
- Sizes line up across components: a 32px input sits next to a 32px button. Corners nest, numbers don't jump, motion is short and stops for `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Small moments carry the character: an empty list with a next step, an error that says how to fix it, a confirmation that says what happens next. Delight comes from the task going well, not from decoration.
- Release is not the end. Changes follow semantic versioning, and the bar keeps rising.

## Influences

These principles draw on Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, Dieter Rams' ten principles for good design, Jakob Nielsen's usability heuristics, the Gestalt principles, the Laws of UX, Microsoft's inclusive design, and the design principles of Shopify Polaris, IBM Carbon, Atlassian, Google Material and Vercel Geist.
